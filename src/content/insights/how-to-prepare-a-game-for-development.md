<h2>1. Describe the core loop in one paragraph</h2>
<p>Explain what the player does every minute, what they are working towards and why they come back. If the loop is unclear, every later decision becomes a guess.</p>
<h2>2. List platforms and minimum devices</h2>
<p>“Mobile” is not enough. Name iOS, Android, PC, WebGL or consoles, and the oldest device you want to support. Performance targets change architecture, art budgets and cost.</p>
<h2>3. Separate must-have from nice-to-have</h2>
<p>Write two lists: features the first release cannot ship without, and features that can follow later. This lets a studio estimate a realistic first version instead of everything at once.</p>
<h2>4. Be specific about online features</h2>
<p>Multiplayer, accounts, leaderboards, cloud saves, live events and purchases each add backend work. State which you need and whether matches are real-time, turn-based or asynchronous.</p>
<h2>5. Share references</h2>
<p>Name two or three existing games that are close to what you want, and say what you like about each. References communicate more than pages of description.</p>
<h2>6. Explain what already exists</h2>
<p>If there is a prototype, art, code or a previous team, share it. Say what state it is in and whether it should be reused or replaced.</p>
<h2>7. State constraints honestly</h2>
<p>Budget range, deadline and any fixed dates such as a publisher milestone or event help a studio propose the right scope. A range is enough to start.</p>
<h2>What to send</h2>
<ul><li>A short game design document or pitch deck</li><li>Target platforms and devices</li><li>Must-have feature list</li><li>Reference games</li><li>Any existing builds or assets</li><li>Timeline and budget range</li></ul>
<p>You do not need a perfect document. A clear two-page brief is far more useful than a long document that avoids the hard decisions.</p>
